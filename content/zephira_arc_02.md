The shrine warmed during the day and cooled sharply at night. Hunger became a hollow ache. Her tongue felt too large and dry. Muscles cramped in cycles despite the changing postures: first one calf, then the small of her back, then a hard knot beneath the shoulder forced open by her bound arms. She learned to breathe around each one. She learned which small shifts the rope allowed and how to use them. She learned that a powerful body could endure much and complain constantly without contradiction.

The blue disk remained dark beside the unreachable water.

She was not ready.

Not because requesting help would constitute defeat. She had no wager to lose and no opponent to impress. The session had begun as hers. Even after the mistake, the hours remained private in a way she valued. Rope, strain, arousal, thirst and the ridiculous bronze reflection belonged entirely within a circuit of giver and receiver that closed through Zephira alone.

She had selected the setting. She was suffering it. No one else had authored the unfairness.

The fact still turned her on.

By the fourth night, the absurdity had worn thin enough for loneliness to show through.

That surprised her more than fear would have. She had spent nights alone in deserts, tombs and jungles with only weapons for company. Solitude usually felt clean. This did not. The restraint made every discomfort intimate, and there was nobody to hear the small remarks with which she normally kept herself company. Talking to the artifact had become undignified even by generous standards.

She thought of Cordelia standing at the Lost City threshold, gaze respectful and interested. She remembered Emily accepting the boundary without floating through so much as a corner. She remembered the blue glass lying upon an open palm.

Solitary and unreachable are different words.

Zephira looked at the charm.

"Not yet," she said.

The fifth morning arrived colorless through the narrow window.

The artifact turned her face-down. Her arms had gone beyond aching into a heavy, distant discomfort. Rope supported most of her torso but left her breasts hanging between two lines, swaying faintly whenever the harness moved. One knee folded beneath her while the other leg extended backward, ankle drawn high. The posture looked indecently graceful in the bronze shield. Even exhausted, thirsty and furious, Zephira felt the involuntary warmth of seeing herself arranged so completely.

"Still cute," she whispered.

The declaration became a laugh, then something too close to a sob for her patience.

Her body had become a map of rope: dark red crossings, bronze skin, shallow impressions and sweat dried into salt. Every muscle felt used without having accomplished anything. She could survive longer. She could wait for thirst to kill her, wake in Lut Gholein and claim with technical accuracy that she had escaped without assistance.

The prospect was idiotic.

More importantly, she no longer wanted to be the only person in the world who knew where she was.

Zephira closed her eyes.

She did not call Cordelia's name. Pride still resisted the shape of a direct request, and the charm had never demanded one. She simply let the wish move outward—the sincere, unguarded desire that someone, anyone, hear her now.

The blue glass rang once.

The call left her without changing anything else in the shrine.

Zephira exhaled against the rope beneath her chest. Relief arrived before rescue, complicated by embarrassment and by a darker thrill she had not expected. Somewhere beyond the shrine, Cordelia now knew that Zephira had chosen to be found.

The artifact changed posture again.

Rope tightened around her waist and began to turn her slowly upright.

"Take your time," Zephira muttered toward the empty air. "I have three weeks."

## Chapter Six: You Asked Me To

Cordelia arrived before the artifact finished turning her.

One instant the ruined shrine contained rope, dry light and Zephira's increasingly strained relationship with her own judgment. The next, blue-white magic folded open beside the platform and deposited a tall sorceress in travel clothes upon the mosaic.

Cordelia had apparently been interrupted at breakfast. Her heavy black hair was tied with a plain ribbon instead of its usual traveling braid. One boot was properly fastened and the other was not. She carried her staff in one hand and a piece of bread in the other.

Neither fact made Zephira feel less exposed.

Cordelia looked at her once.

The glance was enough to take in the four glowing crescents, the rope marks, the slack water skin, the food left untouched and the difference between an intentional ordeal still being enjoyed and one which had gone on long past its intended end. Concern sharpened her amber-brown eyes. It did not turn into alarm, accusation or appetite.

"Good morning," she said.

Zephira's voice emerged rough. "Debatable."

Cordelia crossed to the pack, set down the bread and uncorked the nearest water skin. Then she stopped with it in hand.

"May I end the session?"

The question should have been easy. Zephira had reached outward. She had summoned help. Still, some stubborn fragment of the woman who had lasted five days wanted to qualify the answer, explain the notation, blame Drognan and establish that she would eventually have solved the problem by dying with remarkable efficiency.

The rope rolled her upright.

Bound arms tightened behind her. A supporting line settled beneath breasts made sore by days of pressure. Her extended leg swung, strong toes curling in empty air while the first clean water she had seen since beginning remained in Cordelia's hand.

"Yes," Zephira said.

Cordelia nodded once.

"Emily?"

The ghost emerged from the piece of bread.

"I was accompanying the breakfast," she explained.

Zephira closed her eyes. "Naturally."

Emily's expression changed when she saw the dial. Humor remained available somewhere, but concern took precedence. She floated close without touching either woman and read the selected rings from a respectful angle.

"Four weeks," she said.

"That was not the intended number."

"No, I gathered that from the call."

Cordelia looked at her. "The call carried no explanation."

"I gathered it from the Askari who appears ready to murder a symbol."

Zephira tried to glare at both of them. Exhaustion softened the effect.

"Can you open it?" Cordelia asked.

"Yes." Emily pointed to the two inner rings. "Turn those against one another, press the duration window and tell it the maker recalls the work."

"That is an early release."

"That is an administrative recall. Early release would violate the selected term."

"Your enchantments respect bureaucracy more than suffering."

"That was how I obtained funding."

Cordelia placed the water skin within Zephira's sight, where she could not mistake what would follow, then knelt beside the silver dial. She performed the sequence exactly. The artifact asked for its maker's mark. Emily laid a translucent finger through the window.

Three loops appeared in blue light.

The ropes loosened all at once.

Cordelia caught Zephira before gravity could make the rescue insulting.

There was nothing graceful about returning weight to limbs which had not borne it for days. Zephira's knees folded. Her feet touched stone and immediately objected; cramped calves pulled tight, her shoulders burned as her arms came forward, and the deep impressions around ribs and hips seemed to awaken together. Cordelia took most of her weight without comment, one arm secure around her back and the other beneath her thighs, and lowered her onto the blanket beside the platform.

Rope slid away in long red coils.

The absence felt almost as intense as the restraint. Air reached skin that had learned the shape of every band. Zephira's wrists separated but remained reluctant to move. Her shoulders tried to preserve the bound posture. Strong legs lay heavily across the blanket, muscles twitching as they rediscovered freedom. Without the deliberate geometry of the harness, she felt oddly shapeless.

Cordelia wrapped her in the red sash and folded travel cloak. Only then did she lift the water.

"Slowly."

Zephira drank too quickly. Cordelia lowered the skin before she could choke, waited while she swallowed and offered it again.

The second mouthful tasted better than every wine Zephira had ever lied about appreciating. The third made her eyes sting. She drank in small measures while Cordelia supported the back of her neck and Emily gathered the released rope into quiet loops.

No one congratulated her endurance. No one told her the mistake had been foolish. Cordelia did not mention the naked, bound body she had found or the evidence of several days written across it. Her attention stayed upon immediate ordinary things: water, shade, whether Zephira could move her fingers, whether the cloak beneath her head needed folding again.

Practical kindness dismantled more of Zephira's composure than ridicule would have.

"There were dates," she said eventually.

Cordelia glanced toward the plate.

"I noticed."

"I placed them there."

"A tactical error."

"The water too."

"Ambitious."

Zephira laughed once. It hurt her ribs and came out dangerously close to a broken sound.

Cordelia gave her another drink.

After half an hour, Zephira managed half a date softened in water. After an hour, she could sit without Cordelia's arm behind her. Emily examined the duration dial across the chamber and announced that the mechanism had performed every instruction flawlessly.

"Do not praise it where I can hear," Zephira said.

"I am condemning the operator."

"That is better."

The sun moved across the window. Cordelia stayed.

She spoke about ordinary things while Zephira's body returned by degrees: an amulet Charsi had repaired, a run in the Pit which produced four green items and none worth carrying, the Summoner's growing refusal to maintain dramatic standards. Emily contributed a history of celestial notation which neither living woman requested. The conversation asked nothing of Zephira except occasional attention. It returned the world to a manageable size.

When feeling had sharpened enough to make the rope impressions ache, Cordelia dampened a cloth and wiped salt from Zephira's face, throat and arms. She offered the cloth for everything else. Zephira accepted it, and Cordelia turned her back to give privacy within the open ruin.

That small courtesy made Zephira look at her longer than necessary.

Cordelia had seen everything already. She could have argued that modesty was pointless now. Instead she stood at the window with her dark hair lit blue by the sea and discussed with Emily whether Drognan's translation fee could be recovered through legal threat.

"No," Emily said. "He sold accurate information."

"He sold it to someone who then selected the wrong symbol."

"That improves his defense."

"We can omit that portion."

"Legal scholarship beneath the Zakarum was very particular about omission."

Zephira cleaned herself, dressed slowly with fingers that did not quite obey and crawled the short distance to her armor. By the time she finished, Cordelia had prepared a town portal.

Blue light filled the shrine.

Zephira sat beside it and studied the pattern. Returning to Lut Gholein meant appearing in public. Even wrapped in cloak and sash, she bore rope marks across visible shoulders and wrists. Her stride would advertise the rest. Atma would know. Drognan would calculate. Elzix would be delighted for at least three wrong reasons.

Cordelia waited.

"How did you find me?" Zephira asked.

The question had troubled her since the first flash of teleportation. She knew how the charm worked. Cordelia had described it with almost excessive precision. Yet knowing the mechanism did not explain the emotional fact of being found exactly when privacy turned into loneliness.

Cordelia's answer was quiet.

"You asked me to."

Zephira looked down at the blue disk resting in Cordelia's palm.

She remembered closing her eyes, refusing to name anyone, allowing one sincere wish to leave the sealed circle of herself. The charm had not betrayed her. Neither had the woman carrying it.

"Thank you," she said.

No joke followed. Cordelia simply closed her fingers around the disk and helped Zephira stand.

They entered the portal together.

Atma had converted one of the tavern's rear storerooms into a bathing room after Lut Gholein became prosperous enough for merchants to consider cleanliness a purchasable luxury. Steam softened the tiled walls. A copper basin held more hot water than the entire shrine had contained, and the first sight of it almost brought Zephira to her knees again.

Atma met them at the rear door.

She looked first at Zephira's face, then at Cordelia's supporting arm, the dried rope lines around one wrist and the golden-blonde hair coming loose in dusty strands. Her expression did not change.

"You missed five suppers," she said.

"Keep them warm?"

"I fed them to paying customers."

"Heartless."

"There is broth upstairs." Atma opened the bathing-room door. "And I have informed Elzix that all wagers concerning your death are void."

"Why?"

"You have returned alive."

"Temporary condition."

Atma's mouth curved. "Bath first."

Cordelia set clean clothes within reach of the basin and turned to leave.

Zephira felt the absence before it occurred.

"Stay," she said.

The word came out more abruptly than intended. Cordelia paused.

"Not to help," Zephira added. "Just—stay in the room."

"Of course."

Atma left them tea, broth and a pot of salve, then closed the door. Emily declared that steam interfered with nothing about incorporeality and was ejected through the wall by mutual agreement.

Zephira lowered herself into the basin.

Warm water found every rope mark. She hissed, gripping the rim until the first sharpness dissolved into relief. Cordelia sat with her back against the closed door and drank tea, giving Zephira as much privacy as remaining in the same room permitted.

The arrangement was strangely comfortable. Zephira washed dust from her hair. Blonde strands darkened and floated around strong shoulders. Salt left sun-browned skin; old scars reappeared clean and pale beneath the water. She rubbed gently around wrists and ribs, feeling each indentation the rope had made. The marks pleased her despite everything. They were evidence not only of the mistake but of the original desire, and rescue had not made that desire foolish.

"I liked much of it," she said.

Cordelia did not turn. "I assumed."

"The first four hours."

"Only the first four?"

Zephira considered the fifth afternoon, when the rope had rolled her into a deep arch and her own reflection had remained unreasonably affecting despite thirst and anger.

"Parts of the rest."

"That sounds inconvenient."

"Extremely."

Cordelia sipped tea. "The set has given me considerable experience with inconvenient enjoyment."

There was no confession demanded in return. Zephira leaned back into the copper basin and closed her eyes.

She slept for fourteen hours after the bath.

When she woke, Cordelia was sitting beside the shuttered window with the calling charm upon one knee and Zephira's duration dial upon the other. Emily floated above both, arguing that the crescents were perfectly legible to anyone who had spent twelve years studying pre-Schismatic Horadric notation.

"Then your market consists of six ghosts and Deckard Cain," Cordelia said.

"Seven ghosts."

"One of them cannot read."

"He is an administrator."

Zephira pushed herself upright among Atma's pillows. Her shoulders complained, but they belonged to her again. "Are you changing it?"

"No," Cordelia said. "I am making certain it did not change itself."

"It didn't."

"Emily agrees."

"With the artifact," Emily clarified. "Not necessarily with you."

Cordelia returned the dial. She had cleaned dried salt from its edges and wrapped it in a square of blue cloth. There was no accusation in the care, no suggestion that sensible women should avoid artifacts capable of enforcing their mistakes. She treated it as equipment worth maintaining because Zephira still wanted it.

The blue disk came with it, cool and ordinary against Zephira's palm. Cordelia's mouth curved as memory of the rescue crossed between them. Heat climbed Zephira's face with astonishing efficiency. Being seen had been unavoidable; remembering it while clean, fed and wearing one of Atma's loose nightshirts was somehow worse.

"You could have mentioned the bread less," she said.

"I said nothing about the bread."

"You arrived holding it."

"You interrupted breakfast."

"It made the rescue feel casual."

Cordelia paused. "Would you have preferred armor and a speech?"

Zephira pictured the sorceress entering beneath a column of fire, full Beneficent Attire gleaming, announcing salvation to a woman naked in her own ropes.

"Absolutely not."

"Then breakfast was correct."

Emily reached for the duration dial. Zephira closed her fingers around it before the spectral hand arrived.

"Still mine," she said.

"I was going to mark the four-hour setting."

"I can identify it now."

"Experience is a costly labeling system."

Zephira placed both artifacts upon the bedside table. The movement pulled faintly at her ribs. For one odd instant the ache recalled not the fifth day but the first hour: ropes settling across her skin, weight leaving her feet, the bronze shield showing a strong body made beautifully useless by its own decision.

She still wanted that.

Rescue had not cured her of anything. The realization loosened something she had not known was tense.

Cordelia seemed to read enough of her expression to understand the outline. She stood and collected her cloak.

"I shall be in the Monastery for the next few days," she said. "After that, wherever the Grail ledger is least encouraging."

Zephira turned the blue disk between her fingers. "Even if I select another idiotic setting?"

"Especially then. They make better stories."

Zephira threw a pillow. Cordelia caught it, placed it neatly at the foot of the bed and left before gratitude could become solemn.

The next day was almost entirely food. Atma provided broth, bread, melon and six opinions concerning the wisdom of disappearing without notice. Zephira ate in the quiet corner beneath the stairs while regular customers conducted regular arguments around her. The ordinary noise felt luxurious: dice against wood, cups set down too hard, Meshif accusing a sailor of creative arithmetic.

Elzix came over with a ledger.

"You owe me forty gold."

"For what?"

"I bet Atma you were in a ruin north of the city."

"I was."

"She specified 'unable to reach your trousers.'"

Zephira looked at Atma.

The innkeeper polished a cup without remorse.

"Forty," Elzix repeated.

Zephira paid because challenging the terms would require details.

Three days later, she traveled through the waypoint to the Rogue Monastery to collect the caravan payment rerouted during her absence. The eastern jail had become storage; the cloister held recruits, merchants and two children chasing a chicken which had survived the demonic occupation and thereby acquired unjustified confidence.

Kashya waited near the training ground.

The commander's age showed less in softness than in the economy of her movements. She wore dark leather, a red scarf and the Hellfire Torch at her belt, its small flame catching whenever she turned. Her gaze moved across Zephira with professional completeness: restored stride, fading wrist marks, no visible injury.

"Five days," Kashya said.

"I had a scheduling dispute."

"Atma sent word after the second."

"I was unaware I reported to Lut Gholein hospitality."

"You accepted a caravan contract passing within range of my patrols. When you failed to collect payment, I asked questions."

"Responsible questions?"

"Initially."

Zephira handed her the signed caravan papers. Kashya read them, then looked again at the rope marks disappearing beneath Zephira's sleeve.

"Cordelia found you."

It was not a question. Zephira wondered which detail had traveled: the portal, Atma's bath, Emily's visible presence, or simply the fact that Cordelia had returned to camp one morning with breakfast unfinished and an expression which discouraged interruption.

"She did."

Kashya signed the payment order.

"And the equipment?" she asked.

"Worked perfectly."

The commander's eyes sharpened.

Zephira let her wait.

"I operated it incorrectly," she added.

"For five days."

"It was set for four weeks."

Kashya's control was excellent. Only the fingers resting near the Hellfire Torch tightened. Her gaze flicked once toward Zephira's wrists, then to her shoulders, as though involuntarily constructing the missing arrangement from the marks.

"You could not release yourself."

"That was the advertised function."

"And Cordelia could."

"Emily knew the administrative recall."

Kashya's silence lasted half a breath too long. Curiosity lived inside it—not an invitation, not desire to surrender her own disciplined body, but unmistakable interest in the picture Zephira had not described. The commander appreciated control too deeply to give it away. Perhaps that was precisely why watching its voluntary absence in someone else held her attention.

She stamped the payment order.

"Four hours," Kashya said.

"Lines."

"Not moons."

"You have been speaking with Drognan."

"I can count."

Zephira took the paper. "Would you like to know what the rope looked like?"

Kashya met her eyes without embarrassment. "No."

The answer was immediate enough to be honest.

Then her gaze settled upon the faint crossing visible above Zephira's collar.

"I already know what rope looks like," she added.

Zephira smiled all the way back to the waypoint.

By the end of the week, her body felt normal. The marks faded. Hunger ceased being interesting. Sleep returned to one night at a time. She worked, drank, collected the repaired javelin from Fara and repaid Atma for five theoretical suppers.

The silver duration dial remained locked inside her chest.

So did the red rope.

The blue calling charm sat on top of both.

Zephira opened the chest twice without touching anything. The first time she wanted her spare belt. The second time she had no reason she cared to name.

She looked at the four crescents and felt irritation.

She looked at the four straight rays beside them and felt something warmer.

Cordelia had not made the experience safe. Death already made fatal consequence temporary, and rescue had not erased thirst, cramping or humiliation. She had given Zephira something stranger: a way to let privacy open only when Zephira herself reached for another person.

The distinction remained inside her after every rope mark disappeared.

Zephira closed the chest.

For now.

## Chapter Seven: Someone Knows

For twenty-three days, Zephira became extremely busy.

The caravan master rehired her after extracting a promise that future disappearances would be scheduled around payroll. Fara recovered her javelin, examined the replaced leather grip and declared it both late and adequately repaired. Zephira cleared beetles from a cistern, escorted a jeweler to Kurast, spent two nights aboard Meshif's ship because somebody had begun stealing cargo from the harbor side, and lost enough money at Elzix's tables to restore their relationship to its traditional balance.

She did not open the chest beneath her bed.

This required more effort than any of the work.

The problem was not fear. Fear would have been sensible and therefore easier to dismiss. Zephira knew exactly which error she had made. Four rays meant hours. Four crescents meant weeks. The artifact's obedience had been perfect, and perfect obedience was difficult to blame after the thirst, cramping and humiliating necessity of being rescued had faded.

What unsettled her was wanting the rope again without wanting to repeat the mistake.

Ordinary precautions would solve that. Bring the dial into full light. Read the mark twice. Set water within a position the rope actually permitted her to reach. Choose a shorter arrangement. None of those adjustments changed the familiar warmth that moved through her whenever she pictured dark red lines crossing sun-browned skin.

Yet every imagined final lock now included the moment afterward when she had sincerely wished for somebody else.

That was new.

Zephira refused to think deeply about it because deep thought had never improved an evening.

She drank instead.

"You have been staring at that cup for ten minutes," Atma observed.

"It knows what it did."

"Held wine?"

"Provocatively."

The tavern was full. Lut Gholein had recovered its appetite for noise with astonishing speed. Sailors crowded the long tables; merchants occupied the best cushions and complained that the cushions had become too expensive; two mercenaries near the door were disputing who had killed a leaper neither had actually struck. Music moved beneath conversation from a three-stringed instrument played by a woman whose patience with requests had ended before sunset.

Atma leaned across the bar. "You could simply use the equipment for four hours."

Zephira looked up. "I did not tell you what happened."

"You told Drognan. Drognan told Fara because he wanted an ethical opinion. Fara told me because she wanted to know whether you had been fed."

"A disciplined chain of gossip."

"Public safety."

"It was private."

Atma's expression softened without becoming apologetic. "The details remain so."

That mattered. Everyone knew Zephira had selected the wrong duration and Cordelia had retrieved her. Nobody knew the pattern of the rope, the posture in which she had spent the fifth morning or the involuntary pleasure the artifact had given her while hunger hollowed her stomach. Atma had kept curiosity behind the boundary Zephira never needed to state.

"Four hours," Atma repeated.

"Possibly."

"Lines."

"You have also spoken with Drognan."

"He has received considerable value from this story."

The next morning Cordelia arrived in Lut Gholein to identify a collection of amulets and complain about all of them.

She took the corner table beneath the shutters. The Grail ledger lay open beside breakfast, covered in neat columns and one annotation written so violently that the quill had torn the page. Emily hovered above it, reading each missing item aloud in order of decreasing probability.

Zephira entered after a dawn patrol wearing weathered leather, bronze shoulder guards and her faded red sash. Dust covered both boots. Her thick braid had partly unraveled, and the exertion of running the outer wall had left a warm sheen along her temples and collarbone. She carried a bow in one hand, three recovered arrows in the other and a decision she had spent the entire patrol failing to improve.

Cordelia looked up. "Good morning."

"Also debatable."

Emily emerged farther from the ledger. "Four rays."

Zephira pointed an arrow at her. "Do not."

Atma placed bread, cheese and coffee upon the table before Zephira could order. She added a small dish of sliced melon, looked once between the two adventurers and moved away with an expression of innocence unsupported by history.

Zephira sat.

Conversation remained ordinary for several minutes. Cordelia had found seven amulets in the Worldstone Keep; six protected against poison nobody feared and one granted light radius to a woman who could conjure fire. Zephira's patrol had discovered a breach in the southern wall caused by a camel leaning against old masonry. Emily believed the camel should be appointed to a public office because it had achieved visible results.

Then Zephira placed the blue calling charm beside Cordelia's coffee.

Cordelia's gaze lowered to it. She did not touch the disk.

"I am going to use the ropes tonight," Zephira said.

Emily became still.

"All right," Cordelia said.

"At the old astronomer's tower west of the palace. The upper room. I will begin at moonrise and finish four hours later."

"Four hours."

"Rays."

Cordelia nodded.

Zephira waited for a question. None came.

"I am telling you," she said, "because I want you to know where I am."

Cordelia's attention sharpened by a degree, not misunderstanding but care. "And you do not want me to come."

"Correct."

"Then I shall not."

The simple answer produced an unexpected flicker of heat beneath Zephira's armor. She had anticipated negotiation, perhaps concern after the shrine or an offer to wait outside. Cordelia accepted the shape of the request exactly as given: know, but do not attend.

Zephira pushed the charm back toward herself.

"If I use this, the agreement changes."

"Yes. Otherwise I wait."

Emily raised one hand. "May I ask whether you corrected the lower return?"

"No."

"May I recommend—"

"Also no."

The ghost lowered her hand with scholarly pain.

Zephira ate breakfast. The conversation resumed. Nothing else about the day changed, and that ordinariness made the knowledge feel more intimate. Cordelia knew. She would continue hunting, arguing with Emily and perhaps sleeping before Zephira entered the tower. She would not see the ropes close or hear the sounds they drew from Zephira. Yet the fact would exist in another mind.

By late afternoon, anticipation had sharpened every ordinary sensation.

Leather armor seemed heavier upon her shoulders. The bowstring roughened her fingertips when she returned it to the rack. Bathwater cooled across her skin after training. She washed carefully, rubbed oil into the old scar along her thigh and spent longer than necessary arranging her hair. The practical braid would keep it out of rope. A few loose strands around her temples softened the strong lines of her face in a way she privately liked.

She chose dark blue rope instead of red.

No reason. It looked prettier against her skin.

The astronomer's tower had survived both demons and civic planning. Its lower stairs twisted through abandoned rooms full of cracked lenses, bronze circles and charts depicting stars which had apparently moved out of professional embarrassment. The upper chamber opened to the night through a broken section of dome. Lut Gholein spread below, all warm windows, pale roofs and harbor lanterns reflected in black water.

Remote did not mean invisible. Nobody could see the chamber from the streets, but the city remained near enough to hear: bells, music from Atma's tavern, a distant shouted price, sailors answering one another across the docks.

Zephira liked that.

She laid out her equipment beneath the broken dome. Padded mat. Duration dial. Responsive plate. Silver ankle bands. Blue rope already measured and coiled. A padded bronze spreader bar rested across the far half of the mat, with ankle cuffs at its ends and a paired set of wrist cuffs close together at its center. Beside it waited a shorter articulated bar ending in a low padded neck ring. Two water skins, this time connected to a long drinking tube fixed where any selected posture could reach it. Bread and fruit remained in her pack because she had no intention of being here long enough to need either.

She placed the calling charm upon a narrow shelf.

It remained dark.

She stripped beneath the stars.

Moonlight silvered her sun-bronzed shoulders, the soft curves of her breasts and hips, and the quiet strength beneath her abdomen and thighs. Her reflection waited in a tall, tarnished astronomical plate angled against the wall. Naked, unbound and carrying no weapon, she looked less vulnerable than merely prepared. The transformation had not begun.

Zephira sat cross-legged upon the mat and fed rope through the responsive plate.

"Four hours," she said.

She lifted the dial directly into moonlight.

Four straight rays.

She checked from three angles, then laughed quietly at herself and checked a fourth.

The inner rings selected a floor arrangement rather than suspension. Zephira wanted to feel weight tonight: knees upon padding, hips held low, the slow discovery that strong legs could not stand once ankle and thigh lines opposed each other. She chose moderate compression, limited responsive motion and one posture change after two hours. For the second posture she selected the two-bar frame and confirmed its unusually honest warning: **NO RESTING CONFIGURATION.**

The silver cuffs enclosed her ankles first.

Blue rope crossed each foot at the instep, leaving heels, arches and toes bare. It wound upward around calves in even diamonds, snug enough that flexing changed the pressure everywhere. Zephira extended one leg to admire the pattern. Strength remained visible inside it. The rope did not make her fragile. It made power ornamental.

"Cute," she decided.

Her reflection agreed.

She folded both legs beneath her and engaged the lower circuit. Rope drew the ankles outward while another line settled around her waist and ran back between her thighs. Her knees remained apart. Her heels could not move beneath her. The first loss of leverage arrived with a slow tightening at the hips, settling her weight into the chosen posture.

Zephira tested it.

Thighs flexed. Ankles pulled. The pattern answered by becoming more exact.

Warmth moved low through her body.

She crossed her wrists behind her.

For the smallest instant before the cuffs closed, the tower seemed to hold its breath with her. Zephira could still stop. Cordelia knew what she intended but not whether she had begun. If Zephira dressed and returned to the tavern, the knowledge would remain no more than a plan changed privately.

She put her hands into the cuffs.

Silver sealed.

The rope enclosed forearms, wrists and elbows in a smooth, methodical progression. Her shoulders drew back. A broad band circled beneath her breasts; another crossed above, framing rather than flattening them. Lines traveled around ribs and waist, joining upper and lower harness until attempting to move either half of her body changed the pressure upon the other.

The final knot formed where no bound hand could reach it.

Zephira inhaled.

Rope answered everywhere.

The familiar pleasure returned so strongly that she laughed. Here it was: texture against clean skin, weight made deliberate, posture imposed by instructions she had given while free. Her strong arms could neither unfold nor reach around her. Her legs, capable of carrying her across half a desert, remained arranged beneath her with knees open and feet displayed behind. She turned toward the metal plate and saw exactly what she had hoped to see.
